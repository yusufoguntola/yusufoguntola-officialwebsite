When I joined AFEX, the engineering team was three people. Today it's over sixty, spread across software engineering, product, data, machine learning, DevOps, design, security and support. Along the way I've learned that most of the hard problems at 10x the size aren't technical — they're about coordination, trust, and knowing what to let go of.

## What breaks first

The first thing to break isn't your architecture — it's your communication. At three people, everyone knows everything by osmosis. At thirty, that stops working and nobody notices until a decision gets made twice, or not at all, because everyone assumed someone else owned it.

The fix isn't more meetings. It's writing things down as a default, not an afterthought — decisions, ownership, and the reasoning behind both.

## You can't stay the best engineer in the room

It's not enough to be a great frontend engineer — you need enough working knowledge of every discipline you lead to ask the right questions, even when you can't write the code yourself anymore. That's a hard trade to make, and it never fully stops feeling uncomfortable.

## Hire for the org you're building, not the one you have

Early hires need to be generalists who can operate with very little process. Later hires need to be able to operate *within* process — and to help build it. Hiring the wrong shape of person for the current stage is one of the more expensive mistakes I've made.

## What stays true at every size

- Ship something small before you commit to the full-blown version. The environment changes faster than a long roadmap can track.
- Give people ownership of outcomes, not just tasks.
- Protect the team's time to think, not just their time to code.

None of this is unique to AFEX or to building for African agriculture specifically — but the stakes of getting it wrong are higher when the system you're building is the thing hundreds of thousands of farmers rely on.
