In modern Islamic finance, equity screening methodologies—such as those published by AAOIFI, Dow Jones, or MSCI—have popularized the notion that a company's stock can be rendered "Shari'ah-compliant" even if the firm engages in interest-bearing debt or earns conventional interest income, provided these remain beneath certain percentage thresholds (e.g., 30% or 33%). 

However, when evaluated strictly through the lens of classical Islamic jurisprudence (*fiqh al-muʿāmalāt*), the foundational contracts governing equity ownership lead to a far strictly defined conclusion. Below is an examination of stock ownership, agency responsibility, and the impermissibility of purchasing shares in corporations tainted by *ribā* (usury/interest).

---

## 1. The Legal Reality of a Share and the Shareholder in *Shari'ah*

To assess whether buying stock is permissible, one must first define what a share represents under Islamic law.

* **What is a Share?** Under corporate law, a share represents a fractional unit of ownership in a legal entity. In classical *fiqh*, contemporary jurists map the joint-stock company to **Shirkah al-ʿInān** (a commercial partnership). Therefore, a share is not merely a financial derivative or a speculative paper ticket; it is an undivided, pro-rata ownership stake (*hiṣṣah*) in the company’s real assets, liabilities, rights, and operations.
* **Who is a Shareholder?** A shareholder is a partner (*sharīk*) in the enterprise. 
* **The Relationship of Agency (*Wakālah*):** Because millions of shareholders cannot manage daily operations directly, they elect a Board of Directors and appoint executive management to run the business. In *fiqh*, this creates a contract of agency (**Wakālah**). Executive management acts as the authorized agent (*wakīl*) performing commercial operations on behalf of the shareholders, who act as the principals (*muwakkilūn*).

Under the classical rules of agency, a foundational legal maxim applies:

> **«لا يَجُوزُ لِلْوَكِيلِ أَنْ يَفْعَلَ مَا لا يَجُوزُ لِلْمُوَكِّلِ فِعْلُهُ»**  
>
> *"An agent cannot execute on behalf of a principal what the principal is legally forbidden to execute directly."*

Stated more closely, scholars of Fiqh will say:

> **«كُلُّ مَا حَرُمَ عَلَى الأَصْلِ فِعْلُهُ حَرُمَ عَلَى النَّائِبِ»**
> 
> *"Everything that is forbidden for the principal to perform is forbidden for the agent/substitute."*

Because a Muslim is strictly prohibited from entering into contracts involving interest, delegating operational authority to executive management does not absolve the shareholder when management issues interest-bearing bonds or opens interest-earning accounts. The legal liability of the agent’s actions binds the moral responsibility of the principal.

---

## 2. The Standard of Knowledge: Acting Upon What You Discover

When evaluating a prospective equity investment, an investor’s obligation follows a simple framework:

1. **Initial Inquiry:** An investor must research the primary business activity of the enterprise. If the primary business is 100% *halal*—such as agriculture, manufacturing, or ethical trade—and shows no evident impermissible operations, the investor may proceed on the assumption of basic permissibility (*al-aṣl fi al-ashyā' al-ibāḥah*).
2. **The Duty of Knowledge:** However, if further inquiry or detailed disclosures reveal that the company’s capital structure includes interest-bearing loans, or that its revenues are partially derived from non-permissible sources (such as treasury interest), **the investor must act upon that knowledge**.

Once an impermissible element (*ḥarām*) is verified within the contract or balance sheet, a Muslim cannot ignore it under the guise of passive investing. Knowledge imposes responsibility.

## 3. Misapplying *Aḍ-Ḍarūrah*: Why Necessity Does Not Sanction *Ribā*

Proponents of quantitative screening often cite the legal maxim of necessity—**"Necessity renders the prohibited permissible"** (*al-ḍarūrāt tubīḥ al-maḥẓūrāt*)—or universal affliction (*ʿumūm al-balwā*) to justify holding shares in leveraged corporations. 

However, classical jurists strictly delineate the boundaries of *ḍarūrah*:

* **Definition of *Ḍarūrah*:** Genuine legal necessity refers to extreme situations where life, limb, basic subsistence, or faith is direly threatened. Wealth accumulation, portfolio growth, and capital appreciation via financial markets fall under the category of needs (*ḥājiyyāt*) or enhancements (*taḥsīniyyāt*), not life-preserving necessities (*ḍarūriyyāt*).
* **The Categorical Prohibition of *Ribā*:** *Ribā* is prohibited by explicit, definitive text (*naṣṣ qāṭiʿ*) in the Qur'an and Sunnah. Classical jurisprudence does not permit engaging in direct interest transactions for commercial gain under the umbrella of investment flexibility. Creating wealth through secondary stock markets does not constitute a valid legal necessity that nullifies the prohibition of *ribā*.

---

## 4. Why Screening Metrics Cannot Render *Ribā*-Tainted Shares Permissible in Classical *Fiqh*

In classical jurisprudence, **no mathematical percentage or ratio screen can transform a contract containing *ribā* into a permissible one.** 

The fundamental objections of classical *fiqh* to quantitative financial screening include:

### A. The Principle of Non-Contamination (*Ikhtilāṭul-Ḥalāl bil-Ḥarām*)
Classical law operates on the principle that when the permissible and the impermissible combine in a single contract, the impermissible element dominates (*idhā ijtamaʿa al-ḥalāl wa al-ḥarām ghalaba al-ḥarām*). A business that is 90% *halal* and 10% *ribā*-financed is not 90% pure; rather, the contract of partnership itself is vitiated (*fāsid*) due to the inclusion of unlawful terms.

### B. The Fallacy of Arbitrary Percentages
Classical texts recognize fixed numerical thresholds only where directly established by divine text (such as *Zakat* rates or inheritance shares). The 30% or 33% financial debt caps used in modern screening are modern juristic approximations (*ijtihād*), not textual imperatives. In classical contract law, an interest-bearing loan of 5% of assets carries the same legal prohibition of *ribā* as one that is 50%.

### C. Financial Purification Does Not Cure Contractual Invalidity
Modern frameworks suggest that investors can "purify" their dividends by calculating the fraction earned from interest and donating it to charity. In classical *fiqh*, purification (*taṭhīr*) is a remedial measure for accidental or unavoidable tainted gains; it is not a pre-execution mechanism to validate entering into an invalid partnership (*shirkah fāsidah*) from the outset. 

---

## Conclusion

From a strict classical *fiqh* stance, a company’s stock remains permissible to purchase **only as long as its core operations and its financial dealings remain entirely free from prohibited elements.** 

While modern screening standards offer a pragmatic compromise aimed at facilitating participation in conventional markets, classical jurisprudence maintains that as long as the elements of *ribā* are present within an entity's operations, no quantitative ratio can alter the core ruling: buying a share means buying into the partnership, and a Muslim cannot be a partner in a contract that yields or pays interest.
